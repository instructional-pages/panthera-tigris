# Trade in tiger parts: history, law, farms

Why people buy tigers and their parts, what the law says, what the seizures show, and the argument
over tiger farms. Poaching is covered factually, without graphic detail (see the brief's policy
limits).

## Terms

- **CITES**: the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora,
  the treaty that governs trade in wild species between countries [@Goodrich2022].
- **Appendix I**: CITES' strictest list. For a species on it, international commercial trade in
  the animals, their parts and products is effectively banned [@Goodrich2022].
- **Tiger range countries (TRCs)**: the 13 Asian countries counted as the tiger's range
  [@Wong2019].
- **Seizure**: an animal or product confiscated by the authorities. Seizures show only the trade
  that was caught, a fraction of the whole [@Wong2019].
- **Tiger farm**: a facility that breeds tigers in large numbers, commercially. CITES Decision
  14.69 prohibits breeding tigers for the purpose of trade [@Wong2019].
- **"Generic" tiger**: a captive tiger of mixed, unknown ancestry, as most privately owned tigers
  in the United States are [@Armstrong2024].

## What tigers are traded for

From the Red List [@Goodrich2022]:

- **Medicine.** In Traditional Chinese Medicine, bones, meat and organs are believed to heal
  various ailments; even whiskers, urine and droppings have been used. In China, bones are
  steeped whole in alcohol as a "tonic" (tiger bone wine); in Vietnam, they are boiled into a glue,
  a piece of which is dissolved in hot water. During COVID-19, tiger products were advertised as
  protecting against infection.
- **Status.** Drinking tiger bone tonic is a status symbol, and tiger wine is given as a gift.
  Skins, claws and canine teeth are prestige display items; skins were reported, anecdotally, to
  be given to senior officers by juniors seeking promotion. These skin customs are thought to
  have declined, because skins are easy to detect, but skins are still traded and worn in western
  China and Tibet.
- **Protection and belief.** Claws and canines are worn as jewellery or as amulets (in parts of
  Thailand, to protect the wearer); in Bangladesh, forest workers believe a piece of tiger bone
  protects against tiger attack.
- **Meat**, eaten by wealthy business people and crime bosses in the Golden Triangle (where
  Myanmar, Laos and Thailand meet).
- **Where the demand is.** Mainly Vietnam and China, rising with wealth. A 2017 study suggested up
  to 11% of people in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City had consumed tiger products. Vietnamese
  communities abroad have carried the habit with them: tiger products were widespread in their
  shops in South Africa in 2019.
- **Substitutes.** Demand for tiger bone has driven trade in lion bones (much of it from legal
  lion farming in South Africa, until a ban), jaguars, leopards and clouded leopards.

## The law

- **CITES.** All tigers have been on Appendix I since 1975, except the Amur tiger, added in 1987.
  International commercial trade has been banned for decades [@Goodrich2022].
- **Enforcement is hard** in places outside government control, such as northern Myanmar on the
  Chinese border [@Goodrich2022].
- **Penalties are weak.** Of 1,167 people arrested in tiger cases in 2000–2018, at least 429 were
  prosecuted; the average jail term, where known, was 3.8 years (8.9 in China, 3.4 in India,
  1.7 in Indonesia). In Vietnam, Indonesia and India, many fines were under USD 500: the profit
  from trafficking far outweighs the risk [@Wong2019].
- **A ban that worked: South Korea.** In the early 1990s South Korea was one of the world's largest
  importers of tiger bone and made tiger medicines. It joined CITES in 1993 and banned commercial
  trade in big cats the next year. Experts surveyed for a 2024 study judged that the trade had shrunk
  substantially, through the ban, strict customs and medicine regulation, and the country growing
  richer. Small illegal sales continue, mainly skins and tiger bone wine, sometimes openly online
  [@ElvesPowell2024].

## What the seizures show

TRAFFIC, the wildlife-trade monitoring network, analysed tiger seizures from 2000 to 2018
[@Wong2019]:

- **At least 2,359 tigers** seized in 1,142 incidents across 32 countries and territories: about
  124 tigers a year. 95% of incidents were in tiger range countries.
- India had the most seizures (463 incidents, 626 tigers), then Thailand (369 tigers) and
  Indonesia (266).
- **Skins** were 40% of seized parts, the equivalent of 1,099 whole skins: about 58 tigers a year
  poached for their skins alone.
- **Whole tigers**, alive (382) and dead (416), rose sharply after 2016, mostly from captive
  sources, largely in Thailand and Vietnam. The biggest single seizure, 187 tigers, was at a
  Thai temple in 2016.
- In 2016–2018, wild tigers seized equalled at least 5.5% of the declared wild population; in
  Indonesia, 11.9%. Because seizures catch only part of the trade, the real loss is probably
  much greater.
- Tigers were often trafficked alongside bear parts, ivory and rhino horn.

## Farms and captive tigers

- **How many.** There are an estimated 15,000–20,000 captive tigers worldwide, at least five times
  the wild population. Less than 1% are in regulated places such as accredited zoos; most are
  farmed or privately owned with little regulation [@Armstrong2024].
- **The argument for farms** is that farmed parts could meet demand and take pressure off wild
  tigers. The evidence runs against it [@Wong2019; @Goodrich2022]:
  - Consumers in Vietnam and China prefer wild tigers over farmed tigers or lions, though they
    will use any of them [@Goodrich2022].
  - Farm tigers leak into illegal trade, and seizures keep happening in the strongholds of wild
    tigers, so there is no sign the farms relieve the pressure [@Wong2019].
  - There have been more reports of tiger farming in the last ten years, and anecdotal reports of
    wild tigers caught alive to stock breeding farms [@Goodrich2022].
  - Laos is a likely source: almost 90% of Vietnam's known trafficking routes came from Laos,
    which has almost no wild tigers left, so the tigers most plausibly came from captive
    facilities [@Wong2019].
- **Europe** has exported live tigers from private collections to countries with breeding
  facilities, and some European facilities have processed captive tigers into products sent to
  Vietnam [@Goodrich2022].
- **United States.** Privately owned "Generic" tigers carry ancestry from all six living
  subspecies. They are neither badly inbred nor especially diverse, and a genetic reference panel
  now lets authorities identify individual captive tigers cheaply [@Armstrong2024].
- TRAFFIC recommends registering every captive tiger with DNA profiles and photographs, and
  closing farms that breed for trade [@Wong2019].

## Disagreements

- **Do farms help wild tigers?** The case for farming (supply undercuts poaching) is not made by
  any source read here; the sources read argue the opposite, with evidence of leakage and
  consumer preference for wild tigers [@Wong2019; @Goodrich2022]. The course presents the
  argument and the evidence against it.
- **Is the trade shrinking?** South Korea shows a consumer country's trade can collapse after a ban
  [@ElvesPowell2024]; Vietnam and China show demand rising with wealth [@Goodrich2022]. Both are
  true in their places; the course teaches the Korean case as what changed it.
