# Culture: history, myth, religion, art

What people have made of the tiger: gods and guardians, symbols of kings, characters in books, and
the national symbol of a conservation programme. Where a belief or custom is described, it is the
people's own account as reported by the source, not a claim that it is true.

## Terms

- **Vahana**: in Hinduism, the animal a deity rides; Durga's is a great cat, often a tiger
  [@PMA101270; @CMA1960].
- **Shakti**: divine creative energy. Lions and tigers both stood for it across India, so artists
  could give Durga either mount [@CMA1960].
- **Four directional animals**: the four creatures of ancient China that guard the four
  directions and their constellations. The white tiger is the west [@Welch2016].
- **Therianthropy**: a person changing into an animal, or an animal that is also a person, as in
  were-tiger beliefs [@Nair2021].

## East Asia

- **King of beasts.** In China the tiger is "king of wild beasts", because the markings on its
  forehead look like the character 王, "king" [@Welch2016].
- **The white tiger of the west.** One of the four directional animals, painted on the sides of
  coffins to protect the dead and keep them oriented in the afterlife. It stands for the element
  metal, which equates with power [@Welch2016].
- **Protector of ordinary people.** When lions arrived from Central Asia around the first century
  AD, together with Buddhism, they took over as guardians of palaces and temples; tigers became
  protectors of common people [@Welch2016]. On the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, thought the
  most dangerous day of the year, court insignia showed the tiger protecting against the "Five
  Poisonous Creatures" [@Welch2016].
- **Emperors as tiger hunters.** The Qing emperors Yongzheng and Qianlong had themselves painted
  hunting tigers [@Welch2016].
- **The tamed tiger in Buddhism.** Buddhist art in China and Japan often shows a calm tiger
  beside a monk or arhat (a guardian of the Buddha's teaching), a picture of a wild force
  subdued [@Welch2016].
- **Korea.** Tiger and leopard skins were tribute to the royal court and diplomatic gifts in the
  Joseon dynasty (1392–1897). Under Japanese rule, tigers were targeted by carnivore-control
  programmes and trophy hunters, and were gone from the southern half of the peninsula by 1924
  [@ElvesPowell2024].
- **Myanmar.** In the Burmese zodiac of the days of the week, a tiger stands for Monday and for
  the east [@Welch2016].

## South Asia

- **Durga.** The Hindu goddess who slays the buffalo demon rides a great cat. Ancient texts call it
  sometimes a lion, sometimes a tiger, sometimes an imaginary creature, and paintings can mix
  the two [@PMA101270]. Painters in the western Himalayan foothills showed it as a tiger
  [@CMA1960].
- **Temples.** Friezes of elephants, lions and tigers decorate many of India's oldest temples,
  including the caves of Ajanta, Ellora and Elephanta [@Welch2016].
- **Tipu's Tiger.** Tipu Sultan, ruler of Mysore (1782–1799), decorated weapons, uniforms and his
  throne with tigers. His life-size mechanical toy of a tiger attacking a European soldier,
  said to express his hatred of British rule, is in the Victoria and Albert Museum, London
  [@Welch2016].
- **Waghoba.** The Warli, an Indigenous people of Maharashtra, worship a big-cat deity,
  Waghoba, at about 150 shrines in one study area. They see tigers and leopards alike as forms
  of Waghoba, and believe that if they keep the rituals, especially the yearly festival of
  Waghbaras, Waghoba protects them from the harm of living beside big cats. An attack is
  understood as the people not keeping their side of the bargain, rather than as the animal's
  fault [@Nair2021].
- **Bonbibi.** In the Sundarbans, Bonbibi, "woman of the forest", is worshipped by Muslims and
  Hindus alike as a protector sent to save people from tigers [@Nair2021].
- **Kinship taboos.** In the Dibang Valley of north-east India, a belief that tigers and people are
  brothers, and taboos about the harm of killing one, shape how people live with tigers
  [@Nair2021].

## South-East Asia

- **Sumatra.** The Kerinci and Minangkabau peoples have lost kin to tigers for centuries but hold
  that ancestral souls live in tigers, which guard customary law; some believe people can become
  were-tigers. Islam, which forbids eating animals that hunt with claws, has also been linked to
  tolerance [@Struebig2018]. Tiger-related deaths have been seen as punishment for broken moral
  codes, not only the tiger's doing [@Nair2021].
- **Thailand.** Tiger tattoos are popular as talismans, and statues of tigers guard Buddhist
  temples as spiritual protectors [@Welch2016]. Some "tiger temples" bred tigers and sold their
  parts (see [trade](trade.md)) [@Welch2016; @Wong2019].
- **Amulets.** Across the range, claws and canines are worn as charms against harm, including
  against tigers themselves (see [trade](trade.md)) [@Goodrich2022].
- **Singapore.** Tigers were common enough in the 19th century that the government paid a bounty
  for each one killed; the last wild tiger on the island was reportedly shot in October 1930.
  Tiger Balm and Tiger Beer (1932) made the animal a brand [@Welch2016].

## Europe and the wider world

- **"The Tyger"**, William Blake's poem in *Songs of Innocence and of Experience*, asks what maker
  could "frame thy fearful symmetry", and "Did He who made the lamb make thee?" [@Blake].
- **Shere Khan.** In Rudyard Kipling's *The Jungle Book*, the tiger Shere Khan has been lame in
  one foot from birth, "that is why he has only killed cattle". The wolves resent him because
  the angry villagers will hunt through the jungle and set the grass alight [@Kipling]. The lame
  tiger echoes what field studies later found: many tigers that attack people are old, injured
  or deformed (see [people](people.md)).

## The tiger as a national cause

- **Project Tiger.** India launched Project Tiger in April 1973, to keep a viable tiger
  population "for scientific, economic, aesthetic, cultural and ecological values" and to
  preserve areas of biological importance as national heritage. It still funds India's tiger
  reserves [@NTCAProjectTiger].
- **A flagship.** Conservation calls tigers flagship species and cultural icons, used to win
  support for protecting whole forests [@Struebig2018].
- **Beliefs help conservation.** Around Kerinci Seblat, people who saw tigers as important to
  their spiritual well-being were more likely to want more tigers [@Struebig2018]; the Warli's
  Waghoba relationship is proposed as one reason people and big cats share land there
  [@Nair2021].

## Disagreements

- **Tiger or lion?** Durga's mount is shown as either; museums describe the same goddess with a
  tiger [@CMA1960] or a mount that mixes both [@PMA101270]. The course says "a great cat, often a
  tiger".
- **Are beliefs tied to ethnic groups?** Anthropologists have linked tolerance of tigers in
  Sumatra to the spiritual beliefs of particular peoples; a survey of 2,386 villagers found
  spiritual beliefs mattered across ethnic groups, not only in some [@Struebig2018]. The course
  teaches the survey's finding and names it as one landscape.
