# Conservation: status, numbers, threats, recovery

## Terms

- **IUCN Red List**: the world's reference list of how close species are to extinction, run by the
  International Union for Conservation of Nature. Its categories run Least Concern, Near
  Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered, Extinct in the Wild, Extinct
  [@Goodrich2022].
- **Assessment**: the Red List's written case for a species' category, by named experts and
  reviewers. The tiger's current one is dated December 2021 and published in 2022
  [@Goodrich2022].
- **Mature individuals**: animals old enough to breed. The Red List counts these, not all animals
  [@Goodrich2022].
- **Generation length**: the average age of parents. For tigers 7–10 years, so "three generations"
  means about 21–30 years [@Goodrich2022].
- **Camera trapping with capture–recapture**: cameras photograph passing tigers; each is
  recognised by its stripes, which are unique, and the pattern of who is photographed again
  estimates how many were never photographed [@Karanth1995; @Duangchantrasiri2016].
- **Source site**: a protected area with breeding tigers (more than 25 breeding females) inside a
  larger landscape that could hold more than 50; the places a recovery can grow from
  [@Goodrich2022].

## Status

- The tiger is **Endangered** (criterion A2abcd). It has been listed as Endangered in every
  assessment since 1986 [@Goodrich2022].
- The assessors call the choice of Endangered over Vulnerable "marginal". Depending on which
  1990s estimate it is compared with, the population fell by 22–68% over three generations. Of
  nine possible comparisons, five give Endangered, and the IUCN guidelines ask for caution
  [@Goodrich2022].
- The range shrank by more than half over the same period (see [range](range.md)), and tigers
  vanished from Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos [@Goodrich2022].
- The current population trend is listed as **decreasing** [@Goodrich2022].

## How many tigers

- **3,726–5,578 wild tigers** (not counting cubs), in ten countries. Of these, 2,608–3,905 are
  mature, with a best estimate of 3,140 [@Goodrich2022].
- The assessors call this the first reasonably rigorous global estimate, built mostly from camera
  trapping and occupancy surveys, and a baseline for the future. They warn explicitly that it
  **cannot be read as an increase** over earlier Red List figures, which were rough and covered
  the range poorly [@Goodrich2022].
- South Asia holds 76% of the world's tigers [@Goodrich2022].
- National counts mix methods and years (Bhutan's is from 2014; Indonesia's from 2004–2018
  data), and tigers that cross borders may be counted twice [@Goodrich2022].

**India**, counted every four years by the National Tiger Conservation Authority and the Wildlife
Institute of India [@NTCA2023]:

| Year | Estimate (range) |
| --- | --- |
| 2006 | 1,411 (1,165–1,657) |
| 2010 | 1,706 (1,507–1,896) |
| 2014 | 2,226 (1,945–2,491) |
| 2018 | 2,967 (2,603–3,346) |
| 2022 | 3,682 (3,167–3,925) |

- The lower bound of each range is the minimum number of different tigers actually photographed
  [@NTCA2023].
- India estimates that it holds about 75% of the world's wild tigers [@NTCA2023].
- About 65% of India's 2018 total lived in Tiger Reserves [@Goodrich2022].

**Nepal**, counted every four years by its Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation
[@IUCN2026Nepal]:

- 121 (2009), 198 (2013), 235 (2018), 355 (2022), 429 (2026): more than three times as many in
  about 15 years.
- 2026 by park: Chitwan 145, Bardiya 112, Parsa 71, Banke 51, Shuklaphanta 50. Every park rose
  except Bardiya, down from 125 in 2022.

**A slow recovery: Thailand.** After Thailand set up intensive anti-poaching patrols in 2005 at Huai
Kha Khaeng, its largest tiger population, camera traps from 2006 to 2012 identified 90 tigers.
Numbers swung between 35 and 58, at 1.25–2.01 tigers per 100 km²: 82–90% lower than comparable
sites in India, possibly because of poaching. Patrolling was linked to a small improvement in
survival and recruitment. The authors conclude that low-density populations may recover more
slowly than global plans assume [@Duangchantrasiri2016].

**Decline elsewhere.** Tigers were lost from the only source site in Laos, and they appear to be in
steep decline in Malaysia [@Goodrich2022].

## Threats

From the Red List, in order of the weight it gives them [@Goodrich2022]:

1. **Hunting of tigers and their prey**: the main cause of decline since 1991, and the only threat
   it scores as high impact. Tigers are poached for skins, bones, meat and tonics (see
   [trade](trade.md)). In South-East Asia, snares and poison have emptied forests of
   tigers and prey alike. Poaching was the main cause of death of tigers studied in Russia.
2. **Habitat loss and fragmentation**: forest turned into farms and plantations, logging,
   settlements, and roads and railways cutting through tiger land. Small, cut-off populations
   lose genetic variety and become inbred; one isolated reserve in India now has many
   pseudomelanistic ("black") tigers for this reason (see [body](body.md)) [@Goodrich2022; @Sagar2021].
3. **Conflict with people**: tigers that kill livestock or people are often killed in return. The
   Red List says this is commonly assumed to be important but has never been systematically
   recorded. See [people](people.md).
4. **Disease**: little studied. Canine distemper virus has killed tigers in Russia, and African
   swine fever threatens the wild pigs tigers eat in Russia and Indonesia.

**Why tigers can't absorb much killing.** A modelling study found that tigers need larger
populations than leopards or cougars, are more sensitive to small rises in deaths, and recover
more slowly: they breed later and wait longer between litters. If deaths reach 15% a year,
restoring prey will not be enough (the abstract's words, re-read 2026-11-27: "prey recovery
efforts will not be sufficient if mortality rates reach 15%"). The most urgent step is to reduce
human-caused deaths, especially of resident breeding females [@Chapron2008].

## What is being done

- **The 2010 Tiger Summit.** In St Petersburg, the 13 tiger range countries adopted the Global
  Tiger Recovery Program, aiming to **double wild tiger numbers by 2022**. Its actions: protect
  habitat, end poaching and trade, work across borders, involve local communities, manage
  better, and return tigers to former range [@Goodrich2022].
- India reports that it reached the doubling goal before 2022 [@NTCA2023]. Nepal's count roughly
  tripled [@IUCN2026Nepal].
- **Protection.** Only 21% of tiger landscapes are legally protected, and only about 9% strictly.
  Many protected areas are poorly run, with too little money and enforcement. Over 60% of tiger
  populations are probably in protected areas [@Goodrich2022].
- **Beyond protected areas.** Most potential tiger habitat lies outside them: corridors between
  reserves, working forests, and land shared with people [@Goodrich2022].
- **Reintroduction.** Tigers have been successfully returned to several protected areas in India
  and Russia; returns to Kazakhstan (former Caspian tiger range) and Cambodia are being planned
  [@Goodrich2022].
- **Room to grow.** 226 Restoration Landscapes hold habitat without tigers (see [range](range.md))
  [@Sanderson2023].

## Disagreements

- **Are tigers recovering?** The Red List lists the trend as decreasing, and warns that its higher
  estimate reflects better counting, not more tigers [@Goodrich2022]. National counts show
  strong rises in India and Nepal [@NTCA2023; @IUCN2026Nepal], while South-East Asia lost tigers
  [@Goodrich2022; @Sanderson2023]. The two are compatible: the global trend hides a split
  between regions. The course teaches the split, and why a rise in a count is not always a rise
  in tigers.
- **Nepal's baseline year.** IUCN's story dates the first two counts 2009 and 2013
  [@IUCN2026Nepal]; Nepali press reports give 2010 and 2014 (not cited). The course says
  "about 120 around 2010".
- **India's share of the world's tigers.** India says about 75% [@NTCA2023]; the Red List gives
  76% for all of South Asia [@Goodrich2022], using India's 2018 count of 2,967. The two shares
  come from different years and different totals, so the course gives "about three-quarters" and
  says whose figure it is.
