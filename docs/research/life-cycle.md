# Life cycle: mating, cubs, leaving home, death

## Terms

- **Induced ovulator**: a female who releases eggs only after mating, not on a fixed cycle
  [@SINZ].
- **Oestrus ("heat")**: the short period when a female will mate [@Smith1989].
- **Dispersal**: a young animal leaving the area where it was born to settle somewhere else.
  **Philopatry** is the opposite: staying near home [@Gour2013].
- **Subadult**: a young tiger no longer travelling with its mother but not yet breeding
  [@Miller2013].

## Mating and birth

- Females mature sexually at 3–4 years, males at about 4–5 [@SINZ].
- Mating can happen in any month, but most often in the cooler months, November to April [@SINZ].
- Tigers are induced ovulators [@SINZ].
- Before coming into heat a female marks her territory heavily; males mark more while she is in
  heat [@Smith1989].
- Pregnancy lasts about 100 days. A litter is 1–7 cubs, usually 2–4 [@SINZ].
- A female can breed again once her cubs are independent, at about 2 years; if she loses a
  litter (to starvation or to a male killing the cubs), she can conceive again straight away
  [@SINZ].

## Growing up

- Cubs stay with their mother until they can hunt well, usually at 18–24 months, and are fully
  independent at 2–3 years [@SINZ].
- In Chitwan, Nepal, young tigers became semi-independent when their mother's next litter was
  born, but usually stayed in her area until the new cubs began to travel with her, at about 2
  months old [@Smith1993].
- About half of wild cubs die before they are 2 years old [@SINZ].

## Leaving home

From radio-tracking 36 tigers in Chitwan, following 14 young ones until they settled or died
[@Smith1993]:

- Young tigers dispersed at 19–28 months old.
- Wounds on young tigers before they left suggest that aggression from adults pushed them out;
  after a fight, the young one always left the resident's area.
- **Daughters stay close, sons go far.** Three of four females settled next to their mothers; in
  two cases the mother shifted her territory, handing much of her old one to her daughter. All
  four females set up breeding territories.
- Males went farther and settled in poorer habitat. Eight of ten held temporary territories
  first, and only four of those eight survived.
- Tigers crossed degraded forest but not farmland, and none left the Chitwan region.

A genetic study in Pench Tiger Reserve, central India, found the same pattern from DNA in
droppings and hair: females are related to the females around them (within about 4–7 km), males
are not, and at least one female dispersed 26 km [@Gour2013]. Mothers and young males in Thailand
show it in GPS tracks too: the sons' ranges overlapped their mother's almost completely before
they left, and less and less afterwards [@Liu2024].

## Death

- Of the young that reach independence, only about 40% live to hold a territory and breed
  [@SINZ].
- Adults die young too: being territorial, they fight other tigers [@SINZ].
- For lifespan, see [body](body.md).

## Disagreements

- **Age at independence.** "Cubs stay until 18–24 months" and "full independence at 2–3 years"
  [@SINZ] against dispersal at 19–28 months in Chitwan [@Smith1993]. These measure different
  steps (leaving the mother, then leaving her area). The course teaches the steps in order:
  hunting with mother, semi-independent in her area, then dispersal at roughly 1.5–2.5 years.

- **Primary figures for litter size, birth interval and cub survival.** The Smithsonian page gives
  the numbers above without a study. Reported primary figures, not yet read directly: in Russia,
  litters average about 2.4 cubs, about 21 months between litters, cub mortality 41–47% with at
  least 57% of deaths caused by people (Kerley et al. 2003, *Journal of Mammalogy* 84: 288–298);
  in Chitwan, 34% mortality in the first year and 17% in the second (Smith & McDougal 1991,
  *Conservation Biology* 5: 484–490).
