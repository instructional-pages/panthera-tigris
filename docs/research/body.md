# Body: size, coat and voice

Senses (eyes, hearing, whiskers) and the hunting toolkit (teeth, claws, bite) get their own
topic files later; this file covers size, coat and voice.

## Size

- The tiger is the largest living cat, but size varies by region, and a large lion can be bigger
  than a small tiger [@CATSG].
- The Cat Specialist Group gives, for the species overall: weight 75–325 kg, body length
  150–230 cm, tail 90–110 cm [@CATSG].
- Females of every subspecies tend to be smaller than males [@SINZ; @ADW].
- The largest are Amur tigers: males up to 300 kg and 3 m long; the smallest are Sumatran
  tigers, at most 140 kg and 2.4 m [@SINZ].
- Mainland tigers are larger than Sunda Island tigers [@Kitchener2017].
- In Sumatran tigers, the strongest genomic signal of natural selection lies in a region holding
  *ADH7*, a gene linked to body size [@Liu2018].

## Coat and stripes

- Tigers are the only striped cats: a reddish-orange to yellow-ochre coat with black stripes and
  a white underside [@CATSG].
- Each tiger's stripe pattern is unique, varying in the number and width of stripes and whether
  they split or break into spots [@CATSG; @SINZ].
- That uniqueness lets scientists identify individual tigers from camera-trap photos and estimate
  how many live in an area by "capture–recapture": photographing the same animals again over
  repeated sessions [@Karanth1995].
- Mainland tigers are paler with fewer stripes; Sunda Island tigers are darker with more
  [@Kitchener2017].
- There is a white spot on the back of each ear [@SINZ; @ADW].
- Males have a ruff of longer fur around the face, most pronounced in Sumatran tigers [@CATSG].

## Colour variants

- Coat colour comes from melanin, which has two forms: pheomelanin (red to yellow) and eumelanin
  (black to brown) [@Xu2013].
- **White tigers** are a rare Bengal variant: dark or sepia stripes on white fur, blue eyes, pink
  nose and paw pads. They are not albinos: they lack most pheomelanin but keep eumelanin in the
  eyes and stripes [@Xu2013].
- The cause is a single amino-acid change (A477V) in the gene *SLC45A2*, the same gene behind
  colour variation in humans, horses and chickens. The trait is recessive: a tiger is white only
  with two copies [@Xu2013].
- White tigers were seen now and then in the wild in India, with records back to the 1500s
  [@Xu2013]; the last documented wild one was shot in Bihar in 1958 [@CATSG].
- Captive white tigers are often inbred to keep the trait, and suffer health problems as a result
  [@Xu2013].
- **"Black" (pseudomelanistic) tigers** have wide stripes that merge. About 37% of tigers in
  Similipal Tiger Reserve, a 2,750 km² park in Odisha, eastern India, look like this, and camera
  traps across the tiger's range have found them nowhere else in the wild [@Sagar2021].
- The cause is a change (H454Y) in the gene *Taqpep*, which also shapes tabby markings in house
  cats and the king cheetah's pattern; it is recessive [@Sagar2021].
- The variant was found in 12 Similipal tigers (allele frequency 0.58) and in none of 395 tigers
  from other populations. The likely reason is genetic drift in a small, isolated population
  with low genetic diversity [@Sagar2021].

## Voice

- The roar is a long-distance advertisement call, with an average fundamental frequency (the
  pitch) of 158 Hz, ranging from 83 to 246 Hz [@Klemuk2011].
- Unlike most mammals', tiger and lion vocal folds hold a layer of fat [@Klemuk2011].
- That fat had been thought to lower the pitch by adding mass. Klemuk et al. found instead that
  it gives the vocal folds a large, square shape that makes it easy to start sounding, extending
  the voice's range of loudness, and may protect the folds [@Klemuk2011].

## Lifespan

- Sources differ: 12–15 years [@CATSG]; usually 10–15 in the wild, up to 20 in captivity or
  rarely in the wild [@SINZ]; 8–10 in the wild, up to 26 in captivity [@ADW].

## Disagreements

- **Maximum size.** Figures of 423 kg and 3.7 m for male Amur tigers [@ADW] are far above the
  300 kg and 3 m given by the Smithsonian [@SINZ] and the 325 kg upper bound given by the Cat
  Specialist Group [@CATSG]. Such records are usually old, captive or untraceable. The course
  uses the IUCN range and the Smithsonian maxima until a primary dataset of wild weights is read.
- **Lifespan** (above). The course gives a range and says that wild lifespans are hard to
  measure, until a primary field study is found.
