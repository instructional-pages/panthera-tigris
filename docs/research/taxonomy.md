# Taxonomy: what a tiger is, and how many kinds there are

## The terms the learner needs

- **Taxonomy** sorts living things into nested groups: species within a genus, genera within a
  family. A **subspecies** is a geographically separate population within a species that is
  distinct enough to name.
- A scientific name is *Genus species* (italic), plus a third word for a subspecies: *Panthera
  tigris tigris*. The author and year after a name (Linnaeus, 1758) record who first described
  it [@Kitchener2017].
- A **type locality** is the place the animal a name is based on came from, and the **holotype**
  is that specimen. A name with no description or type is a *nomen nudum* and isn't valid under
  the zoological code; Kitchener et al. use this to reject the name "jacksoni" [@Kitchener2017].

## Where the tiger sits

- The tiger is *Panthera tigris* (Linnaeus, 1758), in the cat family (Felidae) and the genus
  *Panthera*, which has five species and includes the largest living cats [@Kitchener2017].
- The wider group of pantherine cats has seven living species [@Mazak2011]; the two not in
  *Panthera* are the clouded leopards (*Neofelis*), whose subspecies sit just before the tiger in
  the 2017 taxonomy [@Kitchener2017].
- Its closest living relative is probably the snow leopard (*Panthera uncia*): the earliest split
  in *Panthera* separates lion, leopard and jaguar from tiger and snow leopard, supported by the
  Y chromosome, mitochondrial DNA and the combined data [@Davis2010; @Mazak2011]. See
  Disagreements.

## Subspecies: the older view (eight)

Wozencraft (2005), as listed by Kitchener et al., recognised eight subspecies [@Kitchener2017]:

| Name | Common name | Distribution | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *P. t. tigris* | Bengal | India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Nepal | living |
| *P. t. virgata* | Caspian | Caspian region, Central Asia, Tarim Basin | extinct |
| *P. t. altaica* | Amur (Siberian) | Russian Far East, NE China, Korea | living |
| *P. t. sondaica* | Javan | Java | extinct |
| *P. t. amoyensis* | South China | China | extinct in the wild |
| *P. t. balica* | Bali | Bali | extinct |
| *P. t. sumatrae* | Sumatran | Sumatra | living |
| *P. t. corbetti* | Indochinese | Indochina, S China | living |

- Luo et al. (2004) proposed a ninth, the Malayan tiger "*P. t. jacksoni*", from the Malay
  Peninsula, on mitochondrial DNA and microsatellites, and suggested "at least five and possibly
  six" living subspecies [@Luo2004]. Kitchener et al. (2017) call the name a *nomen nudum*, since
  it was published without a description [@Kitchener2017].
- The common names are the usual English ones; the 2017 table gives only scientific names.

## Subspecies: the 2017 IUCN revision (two)

- Earlier studies had found anything from two or three to eight or nine subspecies
  [@Kitchener2017].
- Kitchener et al. followed Wilting et al. (2015), which found no support from morphology,
  molecules and ecology for separate mainland subspecies but did support a separate Sunda Islands
  tiger [@Kitchener2017].
- The revision [@Kitchener2017]:
  - ***P. t. tigris***: mainland Asia, India to Russia, Indochina and the Malay Peninsula
    (absorbs virgata, altaica, amoyensis, corbetti and "jacksoni"). Larger, paler, fewer stripes.
  - ***P. t. sondaica***: Sumatra, formerly Java and Bali (absorbs balica, sumatrae). Smaller,
    darker, more stripes.
- Within mainland *tigris*, Wilting et al. kept northern tigers (former altaica and virgata) as a
  separate management unit because of their distinct body and ecology [@Kitchener2017].
- Evidence used: the Caspian and Amur tigers differ by a single fixed base in 1,257 bases of
  mitochondrial DNA (Driscoll et al. 2009); Sunda Island tigers share haplotypes and show no
  geographic structure (Wilting et al. 2015); skull traits once used to separate subspecies are
  shared or overlap [@Kitchener2017].
- The same report records a dissent: Luo and Driscoll keep amoyensis, tigris and corbetti,
  recognise "jacksoni" and merge altaica with virgata [@Kitchener2017].
- The IUCN SSC Cat Specialist Group's species page says the taxonomy "is currently under review"
  [@CATSG].

## Subspecies: whole genomes (six living)

- Whole genomes from 32 specimens resolve six well-supported groups matching six living
  subspecies: Bengal, Amur, South China, Sumatran, Indochinese and Malayan; gene flow between them
  is very low [@Liu2018].

## Disagreements

- **How many subspecies.** Two (morphology plus a few genes, 2017 [@Kitchener2017]) against six
  living (whole genomes, 2018 [@Liu2018]; and Luo et al.'s mtDNA work [@Luo2004]). The 2018 study
  is the more recent and uses far more of the genome, but the 2017 revision is the IUCN's formal
  classification, and IUCN says it is under review [@CATSG].
  - *Chosen for the course (approved by the owner, 2026-11-27):* teach the disagreement as a lesson in how
    science draws lines. Use the six living groups as names the learner will meet (they are the
    populations conservation work is organised around), and show the two-subspecies view as the
    current formal IUCN classification, with the evidence for each.
- **Closest relative.** The Smithsonian's National Zoo says the tiger's closest relative is the
  lion [@SINZ]; molecular studies put the snow leopard there [@Davis2010; @Mazak2011]. Davis et
  al. also note that different parts of the genome give conflicting trees [@Davis2010]. The course
  follows the molecular studies.
