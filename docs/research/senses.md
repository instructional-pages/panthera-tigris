# Senses: hearing, smell, sight, touch

This topic is thinner than the others: few studies measure a tiger's senses directly, and most
of what is written about them is borrowed from house cats. The file keeps only what a source
says about tigers, and lists the rest under **Open**.

## Terms

- **Cochlea**: the coiled tube of the inner ear that turns sound into nerve signals. Along it runs
  the **basilar membrane**; each point along it responds best to one pitch, high pitches at one
  end and low at the other [@Bergevin2012].
- **Tuning**: how narrow a band of pitches one point of the cochlea responds to. Sharper tuning
  means telling close pitches apart [@Bergevin2012].
- **Hz / kHz**: hertz, vibrations per second, the measure of pitch; 1 kHz is 1,000 Hz.
- **Pupil**: the opening in the eye that lets light in. It can be round, a vertical slit or a
  horizontal slit [@Banks2015].

## Hearing

- Tigers' hearing is built for low sounds. Their basilar membrane is 36–39 mm long, against about
  25 mm in house cats and 35 mm in humans [@Bergevin2012].
- Measuring sounds the ear itself gives off (otoacoustic emissions) in nine ears of five zoo
  tigers, Bergevin et al. found that the tiger's cochlea is tuned about as sharply as a human's,
  and much more sharply than a house cat's [@Bergevin2012].
- The authors suggest that sharp tuning could matter for a tiger's ability to make out the
  features of complex sounds, especially in noisy or echoing places [@Bergevin2012].
- Brain-response measurements suggest the upper limit of a tiger's hearing is lower than a house
  cat's [@Bergevin2012].
- This fits their calls, which carry much of their energy below 1 kHz [@Bergevin2012]; the roar
  averages 158 Hz (see [body](body.md)) [@Klemuk2011].
- Tigers hunt mostly at night, using sight and sound to find prey [@SINZ].

## Human speech, for comparison

- People speak at a lower pitch than many expect. Adult men average about 125 Hz and adult women
  about 200 Hz [@NCVS]; a study of Australian English speakers found 115 Hz for men and 199 Hz for
  women in conversation [@SydneyVoiceLab]. So a tiger's roar (83–246 Hz, average 158 Hz
  [@Klemuk2011]) sits in the same band as human voices: what makes it different is not its pitch
  alone.

## Smell

- Tigers leave messages by scent: urine sprayed on trees and bushes, and scrapes on the ground
  marked with urine, faeces and secretions of the anal glands (see [behaviour](behaviour.md))
  [@Smith1989].
- The fluid a male Bengal tiger sprays is urine mixed with a little fat. The fat holds the
  smelly compounds and releases them slowly, so a mark keeps signalling after the tiger has
  gone [@Burger2008].
- Chemists found 98 volatile compounds in that fluid, plus sulphur, and a protein (cauxin) also
  found in house-cat urine [@Burger2008].

## Sight

- Pupil shape follows how an animal hunts. Among 214 land species, vertical-slit pupils go with
  ambush predators active by day and night, and horizontal pupils with prey animals [@Banks2015].
- The advantage of a vertical slit (judging distance along the ground) is strongest for short
  animals: of the frontal-eyed ambush predators with vertical pupils, 82% stand under 42 cm at
  the shoulder, while only 17% of those with round pupils do [@Banks2015]. A tiger stands far
  taller than that, and so falls on the round-pupil side; that tigers do have round pupils needs
  its own source (see **Open**).
- The counts behind that: of 65 frontal-eyed ambush predators in the database, 44 have vertical
  pupils and 19 circular; 82% of the vertical and 17% of the circular stand under 42 cm at the
  shoulder [@Banks2015]. Worked out (the course's arithmetic, not the paper's): about 36 vertical
  and 3 circular under 42 cm; about 8 vertical and 16 circular at 42 cm or taller.
- Of 42 herbivorous prey animals, 36 have horizontal pupils [@Banks2015].
- Examples the paper shows: the house cat's vertical slit, the lynx's vertically elongated
  (subcircular) pupil, the human's circular pupil and the sheep's horizontal one [@Banks2015].

## Disagreements

- **What gives tiger marks their smell.** Earlier work named 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline as the key odour
  of tiger urine; Burger et al. did not detect it, nor cadaverine or putrescine, in Bengal tiger marking fluid [@Burger2008].
  The earlier studies (Brahmachary and colleagues) have not been read yet. Until they are, the
  course teaches that marking fluid is a long-lasting chemical message and does not name one
  compound as "the" tiger smell.

## Open

- **Vision.** No source read yet on tiger colour vision (cone types), night vision (the tapetum
  lucidum, the reflective layer behind the retina) or pupil shape in tigers. (Checked again
  2026-11-27: a 2015 NPR story quotes Bill Sprague, one of Banks et al.'s authors, saying lions and
  tigers have round pupils. Press is never cited; the paper's species list, table S1, would
  settle it, but it couldn't be reached.) Popular claims that
  tigers see "few colours" and that prey see tigers as green trace to a 2019 *Journal of the
  Royal Society Interface* paper (Fennell et al.) on prey vision, still to read.
- **Whiskers and touch**: no source yet.
- **Flehmen** (the open-mouthed grimace that draws scent to the roof of the mouth) and the
  vomeronasal organ: no tiger source yet.
- **Infrasound.** The Omaha tiger-hearing project (Walsh et al.) is reported to have found
  infrasonic parts (below human hearing) in tiger calls; its papers are not read yet, so the
  claim stays off the site.
- The Brahmachary et al. papers on 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline, and a 2016 *Molecules* paper reporting
  it in Amur tiger marking fluid, to read for the disagreement above.
