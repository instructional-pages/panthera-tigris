# Range: where tigers lived, and where they live now

## Terms

- **Range**: the area where a species lives. The **indigenous range** is where it lived before
  people changed things much; the **extant range** is where it lives now [@Goodrich2022;
  @Sanderson2023].
- **Range state** (or range country): a country with wild tigers in it [@Goodrich2022].
- **Extirpated**: gone from one area, while still alive elsewhere. (**Extinct** is gone
  everywhere.) [@Sanderson2023]
- **Tiger Conservation Landscape (TCL)**: a block of habitat where tigers are known to have lived
  recently, big enough for at least a small population. Roughly, one TCL is one population that
  can still meet and breed [@Goodrich2022; @Sanderson2023].
- **Restoration Landscape**: a block of habitat good enough for tigers, but where they no longer
  live [@Sanderson2023].

## The range they once had

- At its widest, the tiger lived from the Black Sea and eastern Turkey to the Pacific coast of
  Russia, and from the southern edge of the Siberian forest to the islands of Java and Bali
  [@Goodrich2022; @Sanderson2023].
- That indigenous range covers about 11.1 million km², in what are now 30 countries
  [@Sanderson2023].
- The extinct western population (the Caspian tiger) lived along rivers and lakes from western
  China and Central Asia to the Caspian Sea, eastern Turkey and the Tigris and Euphrates valleys
  [@Goodrich2022].

## How they disappeared

Over the last 100 years, tigers vanished from (decades are approximate) [@Goodrich2022]:

| When | Where |
| --- | --- |
| 1930s | Singapore |
| 1940s | Bali |
| 1960s | Java, Hong Kong |
| 1970s | Central Asia |
| 1980s | most of temperate mainland China |
| 1990s | tropical China |
| 2000s | Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia |

The 2023 habitat study dates the last three more closely: Vietnam about 2004, Cambodia about
2008, Laos about 2014 [@Sanderson2023]. The South China tiger has not been seen in the wild
since the 1970s and is possibly extinct; see [taxonomy](taxonomy.md) [@Goodrich2022].

**Why the range shrank, in three layers** [@Sanderson2023]:

1. About 60% of the indigenous range is no longer tiger habitat at all: forest and grassland
   turned into farms, plantations and cities (about 6.7 million km²).
2. Another 23% still has the right vegetation but is too crowded with people, too broken up, or
   too short of prey to be usable.
3. Another 6% is good, quiet habitat with no tigers in it: they were killed, or their prey was,
   until they were gone.

That last layer is why the Red List names poaching of tigers and their prey as the main reason
tigers disappeared from places that still look suitable [@Goodrich2022].

## Where they live now

- Breeding tigers are confirmed in ten countries: Bangladesh, Bhutan, China, India, Indonesia
  (Sumatra), Malaysia (the peninsula), Myanmar, Nepal, Russia and Thailand [@Goodrich2022].
- Tigers in southern and eastern Myanmar probably depend on animals moving in from Thailand
  [@Goodrich2022].
- On 1 January 2020 there were 63 Tiger Conservation Landscapes, covering 911,920 km². That is
  8.2% of the indigenous range [@Sanderson2023].
- The largest, the "Northern Triangle" shared by India, Myanmar, China, Bhutan and Bangladesh,
  is about a third of all TCL area (294,847 km²), but tigers are probably not spread through all
  of it [@Sanderson2023].
- Only about a third of the TCL area had been surveyed in the previous five years
  [@Sanderson2023].
- Over 60% of tiger populations are probably inside protected areas [@Goodrich2022].

**The trend since 2001** [@Sanderson2023]:

- TCL area fell by 11% between 2001 and 2020 (from 1.025 million km²), with the biggest losses
  in Southeast Asia and southern China.
- TCLs grew in India, Nepal, Bhutan, northern China and south-eastern Russia.
- After about 150 years of decline, the habitat tigers could actually use seems to have
  stabilised, at about 16% of the indigenous range.
- 226 Restoration Landscapes hold suitable habitat without tigers. If they had enough prey and
  tigers could reach them, the occupied area could grow by about half.
- After tigers were lost, the remaining habitat was lost faster: about four times faster in Vietnam
  and Laos, and 77% faster in Cambodia. The authors suggest that habitat without tigers is more
  easily degraded and broken up.

**Gaps in knowledge.** In remote parts of Southeast Asia and the Himalaya of India, Nepal and
Bhutan, possibly several hundred thousand km², little is known about whether tigers live and
breed [@Goodrich2022].

## Disagreements

- **How much range is left, and how fast it is shrinking.** The 2022 Red List, using a draft of
  the habitat study, gives 71 landscapes and 978,293 km² occupied in 2020, down 7% since 2001
  [@Goodrich2022]. The published study gives 63 landscapes, 911,920 km², down 11%
  [@Sanderson2023]. The published version is later and is the one the course teaches, noting that
  the numbers moved as the model was finished.
- **"Less than 7%" or "8.2%" of the historical range.** The Red List says tigers occupy less than
  7% of their historical range [@Goodrich2022]; the published study puts TCLs at 8.2% of the
  indigenous range [@Sanderson2023]. Both describe a loss of over 90%; the difference is in what
  is counted as the historical range. The course says "less than a tenth".
- **Long-term decline figures.** Earlier mappings gave 1.64 million km² in 1994 (159 units, not
  counting Russia and China) and 1.1 million km² in 2006 (76 landscapes); the Red List compares
  them to infer a 54% decline since 1994 [@Goodrich2022]. It also says part of that apparent
  decline comes from better maps, not fewer tigers [@Goodrich2022]. The course shows the trend,
  not a precise percentage.
