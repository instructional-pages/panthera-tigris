# Behaviour: territory, scent marking, communication

## Terms

- **Home range**: the area an animal uses in its normal life. A **territory** is a home range, or
  part of one, that the animal keeps others of its own sex out of [@Smith1989; @Liu2024].
- **Solitary**: living alone rather than in groups. Tigers are solitary except when mating or
  raising young [@SINZ].
- **Scent marking**: leaving smells (urine, faeces, gland secretions) or scratches where other
  tigers will find them [@Smith1989].

## A solitary life

- Adult tigers live alone; the only lasting groups are a mother with her cubs, and a male and
  female stay together only to mate [@SINZ].
- Being solitary does not mean being out of touch. Tigers communicate by voice (roaring, grunting
  and chuffing) and by signals left behind: scent marks and scratches on trees [@SINZ].
- They are strongly territorial, and these signals tell where one tiger's range ends and
  another's begins [@SINZ].

## Territory

- GPS tracking of five Indochinese tigers in western Thailand (a mother, her two sons, a resident
  male and a neighbouring female) shows the pattern: the three males' ranges overlapped each other
  less than the resident male's overlapped the mother's, and he tended to avoid the young males
  [@Liu2024].
- Two neighbouring females there had ranges that touched along a shared boundary but rarely used
  it at the same time; visiting the edge at different times lets females keep a boundary without
  fighting [@Liu2024].
- Adjacent males also avoid each other, until a female comes into heat near their boundary
  [@Liu2024].
- A female's home range size depends on how much prey there is [@Liu2024].
- Across the range, adult females' home ranges seldom overlap, while a male's range typically
  overlaps those of 1–3 females, the usual pattern in solitary cats [@Goodrich2022].
- Range size follows prey: females in Chitwan, Nepal, averaged about 20 km², in the Russian Far
  East about 400 km² [@Goodrich2022]. See [ecology](ecology.md).

## Scent marking

From four years of watching radio-collared and known tigers in Chitwan, Nepal [@Smith1989]:

- Tigers mark in five ways: **spraying urine** (on trees and bushes), **scraping** the ground and
  leaving urine, faeces or anal-gland secretion in the scrape, **clawing** trees, **rubbing their
  cheeks** on objects, and **flattening vegetation**. Spraying and scraping were the most common.
- Tigers mark more at the edges of their territories than inside them, and most of all where main
  travel routes cross a boundary. They do this by marking more often there, not by spending more
  time there.
- Marking was most intense while a tiger was establishing a territory, and neighbours seemed to
  mark in answer to each other.
- Females marked heavily just before coming into heat, and less during it; males marked more
  when a female was in heat.
- Smith et al. propose that the marks form an "odour field" that tells a tiger how likely it is to
  meet another, so it can weigh the risk of an encounter against the value of using the area.
- Marks can last: the fat in sprayed fluid releases its scent slowly (see [senses](senses.md))
  [@Burger2008].

## Disagreements

- None recorded yet. The two field studies read (Nepal and Thailand) agree on the picture of
  solitary, territorial tigers that keep apart by signals.
