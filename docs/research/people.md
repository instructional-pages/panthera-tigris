# Tigers and people: conflict and attacks

How tigers and the people who live beside them harm each other, and what reduces it. Written
factually, without graphic detail (see the brief's policy limits).

## Terms

- **Human–tiger conflict**: tigers attacking people or their domestic animals, and people killing
  tigers in return. Attacks on people and livestock are its main forms [@Goodrich2010].
- **Livestock depredation**: a predator killing domestic animals (cattle, goats, buffalo).
  Across wild cats, it is a more common cause of conflict than attacks on people
  [@Inskip2009].
- **Retaliatory killing**: people killing a tiger because it attacked them, their family or their
  animals, or because they fear it will [@Goodrich2022; @Inskip2009].
- **Problem tiger** (or "conflict-causing tiger"): one with a history of attacking livestock or
  people. A **man-eater** is one that attacks people repeatedly [@Shrestha2026].
- **Buffer zone**: the belt of forest, farms and villages around a national park, where people
  live and use the forest under rules meant to protect the park [@Dhakal2026].
- **Tolerance**: how far people accept living near an animal. Intolerance shows in acts such as
  killing it or opposing its protection [@Struebig2018].

## Why tigers and people collide

- Tigers need large, intact habitat with enough prey. Habitat loss, prey depletion, villages
  built next to forest, and new roads and other infrastructure bring tigers and people into
  closer contact [@Dhakal2026].
- **Success brings more conflict.** As tiger numbers recover, encounters, livestock losses and
  retaliatory killing are expected to rise; a 2010 review warned that the plan to double wild
  tigers would increase conflict unless it was planned for [@Goodrich2010]. Nepal, which
  roughly tripled its tigers (see [conservation](conservation.md)), now sees exactly this
  concern [@Dhakal2026].
- Tigers sometimes live in places people made, such as sugarcane and oil-palm plantations that
  resemble their habitat, which often raises conflict [@Goodrich2022].
- Across all wild cats, only species heavier than about 50 kg attack people with any frequency;
  leopards, lions and tigers cause most attacks. The tiger is one of nine cat species whose
  conflict with people matters for their conservation [@Inskip2009].

## Attacks on people

**How many.** Counts are local and incomplete, so there is no reliable world figure.

- India: 320 people killed by tigers from 2014 to 2020 (a newspaper figure quoted by the Red
  List) [@Goodrich2022].
- The Sundarbans mangroves (Bangladesh and India): at least 40 people killed in 2000–2010
  [@Goodrich2022].
- Nepal, the Terai lowlands: 245 attacks in 2010–2024, of which 141 were fatal, about 16 attacks
  a year [@Dhakal2026].
- Chitwan National Park, Nepal: 36 tigers killed 88 people from 1979 to 2006 [@Gurung2008].
- Attacks across wild cats have generally declined over the past century, probably because
  there are fewer cats, but they remain common in parts of Asia and Africa [@Inskip2009].

**Where and when.**

- Most happen in or near forest, while people gather grass, fodder, firewood and wild foods, or
  herd livestock. In Nepal, more than 90% of attacks were inside forests, and over half took
  place during fodder and firewood collection [@Dhakal2026]. In Chitwan, two-thirds of
  killings were within 1 km of the forest edge, and nearly half the people killed were
  grass or fodder collectors [@Gurung2008].
- Most were in buffer zones, not inside parks: 69% in Nepal's buffer zones, 24% outside
  protected areas, 6.5% inside national parks [@Dhakal2026].
- Most were by day, matching when people are in the forest [@Dhakal2026; @Shrestha2026].

**Who is at risk.**

- People alone. In Chitwan and Parsa, 69% of victims were alone [@Shrestha2026]. In Nepal as a
  whole, being in a group was the strongest factor reducing the chance of death; companions
  have driven tigers off by shouting or with sticks and tools [@Dhakal2026; @Shrestha2026].
- People bending or sitting. 75% of people attacked while bending or sitting died, against 41%
  of those standing or walking [@Dhakal2026]. Why posture matters was not tested.
- Men were attacked more often (65%), but a larger share of women died (73%) [@Dhakal2026].

**Which tigers.**

- Most attacks are predation attempts, usually ambushes from behind; about a fifth followed
  people provoking or surprising a tiger [@Shrestha2026].
- Old, injured and young dispersing tigers are over-represented [@Shrestha2026]. In Chitwan,
  56% of the human-killing tigers that were examined had physical deformities, and males and
  females killed people equally [@Gurung2008].
- Tigers may also attack defensively, to protect themselves or their cubs, particularly where
  people persecute them [@Dhakal2026].

**Changes over time.** In Chitwan, deaths rose from about 1.2 a year before 1998 to 7.2 a year in
1998–2006, mainly a tenfold rise in the buffer zone after its forests were restored
[@Gurung2008]. Around Chitwan and Parsa, casualties (deaths and injuries) rose from 4.6 a year
(2014/15–2018/19) to 11.4 a year (2019/20–2023/24) [@Shrestha2026]. Across Nepal, attacks peaked
in 2022, after COVID-19 sent many people back into the forest for a living, and fell after 2023
once problem tigers were removed and awareness campaigns ran [@Dhakal2026].

## Attacks on livestock

- Tigers and leopards caused more than 90% of livestock losses to wildlife around Chitwan in
  2012–2016 (a study quoted in [@Shrestha2026]).
- Losing livestock threatens the income of farming families, and the fear of attack adds
  stress and stigma [@Dhakal2026].
- **A tiger can also protect a farm.** In western Bhutan, where a tiger lived in the forest near
  a village, leopards and dholes kept closer to the fields and ate more of the wild animals
  that raid crops, and farmers lost significantly fewer crops and livestock. Where no tiger was
  present, leopards and dholes stayed deeper in the forest and killed more free-ranging
  livestock [@Thinley2018].

## Tigers killed by people

- Retaliatory killing is common and widely assumed to drive declines, but it has never been
  systematically recorded [@Goodrich2022].
- In some regions up to half of tiger deaths have been attributed to retaliation (a Russian Far
  East study quoted in [@Inskip2009]).
- Conflict also turns local people against tigers, which reduces support for conserving them
  [@Goodrich2010].
- In Kerinci Seblat, Sumatra, 27 tigers were hunted in 2000–2013 [@Struebig2018].

## Living with tigers

**Tolerance.** In a survey of 2,386 villagers around Kerinci Seblat, 28% wanted fewer or no
tigers, 48% wanted the same number and 19% wanted more. What predicted tolerance was not
ethnicity but attitudes, emotions, social norms and spiritual beliefs about the tiger; adding
where attacks were likely made predictions of tolerance up to 32 times better [@Struebig2018].
Around Chitwan and Parsa, 85% of people whose families or livestock had been attacked still
supported tiger conservation [@Shrestha2026].

**What helps.** Reviews list fencing (including electric), trenches, predator-proof livestock
pens, patrols, compensation, and awareness and training [@Shrestha2026; @Inskip2009]. Only 31%
of conflict-reduction measures for wild cats had been scientifically evaluated by 2009
[@Inskip2009]. In Nepal:

- People attacked, or their families, most often recommended going into the forest in groups
  and staying alert (56%), and avoiding risky places and times (31%). Fewer than 1% asked for
  problem tigers to be killed [@Dhakal2026].
- Capturing 21 conflict-causing tigers around Bardia (2019–2024), behaviour-change campaigns
  and clearing undergrowth in buffer zones were followed by fewer attacks after 2023
  [@Dhakal2026].
- The government pays relief to victims and families, based on records kept by park and forest
  offices [@Dhakal2026].

**Targeting help.** In Sumatra, combining where encounters cluster with how tolerant each village
is would have focused help on villages that had 54% of all attacks, and might have spared at
least 15 of the tigers removed before 2013 [@Struebig2018].

## Disagreements

- **Is conflict rising?** Local studies in Nepal show recent rises [@Shrestha2026; @Dhakal2026],
  while a global review found attacks by wild cats have generally fallen over the last century
  [@Inskip2009]. Both can be true: fewer tigers once meant fewer attacks, and recovering tigers
  in crowded landscapes bring them back. The course teaches the link between recovery and
  conflict.
- **Do more tigers mean more attacks?** Around Chitwan and Parsa, casualties rose with tiger
  numbers, but with only four census points the authors call it indicative, not causal
  [@Shrestha2026]. Across Nepal, tiger numbers alone are called an incomplete predictor:
  how much people depend on the forest matters too [@Dhakal2026]. The course says
  "more tigers in shared land raise the risk; how people use the forest decides much of it".
- **Rising or falling in Chitwan?** One study finds Chitwan's annual deaths fell from 7.2
  (1998–2006) to 4.5 (2010–2024) [@Dhakal2026]; another finds casualties around Chitwan and
  Parsa rose to 11.4 a year after 2019 [@Shrestha2026]. They count different things (deaths
  versus deaths plus injuries) over different areas, and the second explains that earlier
  studies missed cases. The course does not state a Chitwan trend.
