# Hunting: prey, method, how often

## Terms

- **Ungulates**: hoofed mammals: deer, wild pigs, wild cattle [@Miller2013].
- **Electivity (preference)**: whether a predator kills a prey species more often than its share
  of the prey around would predict. Preferred prey are killed more than their numbers alone
  explain [@Hayward2012].
- **Kill rate**: how many animals a predator kills per unit of time, here given as days between
  kills. **Consumption rate**: how many kilograms of meat it eats per day [@Miller2013].
- **GPS cluster**: a group of GPS-collar locations close together in place and time. A tiger
  that stays put for many hours is often on a kill, so researchers walk to clusters to find
  carcasses [@Miller2013].

## How a tiger hunts

- Tigers are ambush predators: they rely on stealth and strength rather than a long chase. The
  striped coat hides them while they wait or stalk; at the right moment they pounce, pull the
  prey down and kill it with a bite to the neck [@SINZ].
- They hunt mostly at night [@SINZ].
- They swim well and have been recorded hunting in water [@SINZ].

## What tigers eat

- Mainly large ungulates such as wild boar and deer; also recorded taking monkeys, buffalo, sloth
  bears, leopards and crocodiles, and livestock where they live near people [@SINZ].
- Across 3,187 kills or scats of 32 prey species from studies range-wide, tigers significantly
  preferred **wild boar and sambar deer**; red deer and barasingha (swamp deer) were probably
  preferred too, but samples were too small to be sure [@Hayward2012].
- Body mass was the only trait that predicted preference: tigers preferred prey of **60–250 kg**,
  about their own weight (a 1:1 predator-to-prey ratio, like other solitary cats)
  [@Hayward2012].
- In the Russian Far East, of 111 kills found by following collared Amur tigers, 27.9% were wild
  boar, 24.3% red deer and 23.4% roe deer. About half of the large ungulates killed were young
  animals [@Miller2013].

## How often a tiger kills and eats

The best-measured case is the Amur tiger, from GPS collars in the Sikhote-Alin region of Russia,
2009–2012 [@Miller2013]:

- The collared tigers killed about **once every 6.5 days** (95% confidence interval 5.9–7.2), or
  about 56 animals a year [@Miller2013].
- That works out to about **8.9 kg of meat a day**, after allowing for the inedible part of each
  carcass and for scavengers [@Miller2013].
- Rates changed with the seasons: a kill every 7.4 days in summer and every 5.7 days in winter.
  Winter kills were more often large prey [@Miller2013].
- After a small kill the next kill came sooner (5.75 days on average) than after a large one
  [@Miller2013].
- These estimates rest on three tigers (two females, one male), and are higher than earlier
  ones based on snow-tracking in winter, partly because GPS finds small kills that snow-tracking
  missed [@Miller2013].
- A tiger can eat up to 34 kg in one night [@SINZ].

## Disagreements

- **How often tigers kill.** The Smithsonian gives "about once a week" [@SINZ]; GPS work on Amur
  tigers gives one kill every 6.5 days, with earlier studies ranging from about 5 to 9 days
  between kills depending on method and region [@Miller2013]. The course teaches roughly once a
  week, and shows that the number depends on prey size and season.

- **Local variation in prey size.** Karanth & Sunquist 1995 (*Journal of Animal Ecology* 64:
  439–450) is summarised elsewhere as finding Nagarahole tigers selecting prey over 176 kg and
  preferring gaur, against the 60–250 kg of the range-wide review [@Hayward2012]. Its abstract
  was seen only through search-engine summaries; read it before using it.
- Sunquist's 1981 Chitwan monograph (*Smithsonian Contributions to Zoology* 336) covers prey,
  kill rates and food intake in lowland Nepal; the Smithsonian repository blocks automated
  downloads.
