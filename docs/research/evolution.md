# Evolution: where tigers came from

## The terms the learner needs

- **Mya / kya**: million / thousand years ago.
- The **Pleistocene** is the epoch of the ice ages. Its earliest stage, the **Gelasian**
  (2.588–1.806 Mya), was moved from the Pliocene into the Pleistocene, so older papers call the
  same rocks "Late Pliocene" [@Mazak2011]. The **Calabrian** is the stage after it [@Mazak2011].
- **Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)** is passed down the mother's line only. Tracing every living
  tiger's mtDNA back to one ancestor gives the **most recent common ancestor (MRCA)**: not the
  first tiger, but the latest ancestor all living tigers share on that line [@Luo2004].
- A **bottleneck** is a time when a population shrinks sharply and loses genetic variety
  [@Luo2004; @Liu2018].
- A **sister taxon** is the closest branch on a family tree [@Mazak2011].

## The big cats

- Molecular data date the split of the *Panthera* lineage from other cats to about 10.8 Mya,
  probably in Southeast Asia, followed by a fast radiation into the living *Panthera* species
  [@Mazak2011].
- Within *Panthera*, tiger and snow leopard may be each other's closest relatives
  [@Davis2010; @Mazak2011].
- The earliest *Panthera* fossils, about 3.8 Mya from East Africa, are fragments of a lion-sized
  and a leopard-sized cat whose identity is still open [@Mazak2011].
- Fossils that can be assigned to a living species' lineage are all younger than about 2 Mya
  [@Mazak2011].

## The tiger lineage

- ***Panthera zdanskyi*** (the "Longdan tiger"): a nearly complete skull from Longdan, Gansu
  Province, north-western China, dated by palaeomagnetism to 2.55–2.16 Mya. It is the oldest
  complete pantherine skull found, and its analysis places it as the sister taxon of the tiger
  [@Mazak2011].
- It was jaguar-sized: about the size of the smallest female modern tigers, though it was male,
  so smaller than any living tiger male [@Mazak2011].
- Even this earliest member of the lineage had a skull much like a modern tiger's, and its skull,
  bite and teeth suggest it already lived much like one; the skull, jaw and teeth evolved at
  different rates over the last two million years [@Mazak2011].
- Tiger evolution trends towards larger size, though not in a straight line [@Mazak2011].
- The oldest fossils definitely from tigers are jaw fragments from the Lower Pleistocene
  (Calabrian) of Lantian, China; the few nearly complete skulls are from the late Middle or Late
  Pleistocene [@Mazak2011]. Comparison sites include Trinil (Java) and Zhoukoudian (Beijing)
  [@Mazak2011].
- By the late Pliocene and early Pleistocene, tigers were widespread in eastern Asia; ice-age
  swings in climate then repeatedly shrank and expanded their range [@Luo2004].

## Modern tigers: a recent bottleneck

- All living tigers' mtDNA traces back to an ancestor 72,000–108,000 years ago (bounds 39,000 and
  157,000), young compared with some other *Panthera* species [@Luo2004].
- Whole genomes also trace all living tigers back to about 110,000 years ago, after a population
  bottleneck [@Liu2018].
- Luo et al. note that their date roughly matches the Toba super-eruption in Sumatra, about
  73,500 years ago, and propose it as a possible cause [@Luo2004].
- The subspecies differences arose afterwards, from isolation, genetic drift and local adaptation
  as habitats repeatedly shrank and expanded over the last 100,000 years [@Kitchener2017;
  @Luo2004].
- Amur tigers carry no mtDNA variation in Luo et al.'s sample, suggesting a further founder event
  or reduction less than 20,000 years ago [@Luo2004].
- Tigers on Sumatra, Java and Bali form one distinctive clade; island tigers may have been cut
  off by rising sea levels in prehistory (Xue et al. 2015, as summarised in [@Kitchener2017]).
- Modern Indian tigers show more population structure and less genetic diversity than museum
  samples, because of local extinctions and drift (Mondol et al. 2013, as summarised in
  [@Kitchener2017]).

## Disagreements

- **Where the tiger lineage began.** North-central China, southern China and northern Siberia
  have all been proposed [@Mazak2011]. The Longdan skull supports north-central/north-western
  China [@Mazak2011]. Luo et al.'s genetics point to northern Indochina and southern China as the
  Pleistocene centre of the tiger's radiation [@Luo2004]. These may answer different questions
  (where the lineage arose about 2.5 Mya, against where today's tigers spread from after the
  bottleneck); the course should say so rather than pick one.
- **Is *P. zdanskyi* its own species?** Later studies are reported to treat it as a synonym of
  *Panthera palaeosinensis*. Not yet checked against a primary source (see Open).
- **Toba as the cause of the bottleneck** is a hypothesis matched by date, not a demonstrated
  cause [@Luo2004]. Teach it as such.
