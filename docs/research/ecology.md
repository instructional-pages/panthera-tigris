# Ecology: habitat, prey, and the other predators

## Terms

- **Habitat**: the kind of place an animal can live in: its vegetation, climate and food
  [@Goodrich2022].
- **Ungulate**: a hoofed mammal, such as deer, wild pig, wild cattle (gaur, banteng) or buffalo.
  Ungulates are most of what a tiger eats; see [hunting](hunting.md) [@Goodrich2022].
- **Density**: how many animals live in a given area, here tigers per 100 km² (a square 10 km
  on each side) [@Karanth2004].
- **Guild**: the species in one place that live on the same kind of food; here tigers, leopards
  and dholes (Asian wild dogs) [@Steinmetz2013].
- **Competition**: when two species need the same thing that is in short supply. **Interference
  competition** is the direct kind: the bigger one chases off, or kills, the smaller
  [@Harihar2011; @Steinmetz2013].

## Where tigers can live

- Tigers are **habitat generalists**: they live in tropical rainforest, mangrove swamps (the
  Sundarbans of India and Bangladesh), the semi-dry forests of western India, Himalayan forests up
  to about 4,500 m, and the temperate forests of Russia and north-east China [@Goodrich2022].
- What they all need is enough large prey. The Red List calls a good population of wild pigs and
  deer the tiger's primary habitat requirement [@Goodrich2022].
- Tigers can use land people have changed, such as sugarcane and oil-palm plantations, especially
  where wild ungulates live there too. This helps tigers survive in a changing landscape, but often
  brings more conflict with people [@Goodrich2022].
- Breeding tigers sometimes live at fairly high densities in forest that people graze, log or
  collect from, but such populations are probably less stable, with lower survival
  [@Goodrich2022].

## Prey sets the number of tigers

- **More prey, more tigers.** In 11 sites across India, studied from 1995 to 2003, prey densities
  ranged from 5.3 to 63.8 ungulates per km², and tiger densities from 3.2 to 16.8 per 100 km². A
  simple model that predicts tiger density from prey density fitted these sites reasonably well
  [@Karanth2004].
- Across the whole range, tiger density runs from 15–19 per 100 km² where prey is plentiful (Kaziranga
  and Corbett in India) down to 0.13–0.45 per 100 km² in Russia's Sikhote-Alin mountains
  [@Goodrich2022].
- Home ranges follow the same rule. Females in Chitwan, Nepal, averaged about 20 km²; in the
  Russian Far East, about 400 km² [@Goodrich2022]. See [behaviour](behaviour.md).
- Densities are generally highest on river floodplains and in tropical deciduous forest, and lower
  in evergreen rainforest and in the temperate north [@Goodrich2022].
- A tiger kills roughly 50–60 large prey animals a year (see [hunting](hunting.md))
  [@Goodrich2022; @Miller2013].
- **Where the prey is matters, not just how much.** A computer model of tigers in Nepal's Chitwan
  National Park found that removing prey evenly over a wide area (rather than heavily in a few
  places) made female territories larger and the tiger population smaller [@Carter2019].

## Tigers, leopards and dholes

In much of South and South-East Asia tigers share the forest with leopards and dholes, which hunt
many of the same animals. Several studies show how they manage to live together:

- **By eating different prey.** In Nagarahole, southern India, the three predators killed
  different prey species, sizes and ages, which lets them coexist. They used the same areas and
  were active at similar times; the authors found no sign that tigers kept the others out
  [@Karanth2000].
- **By avoiding tigers where prey is scarce.** In a Thai forest with few prey, leopards and dholes
  were found less often where tigers were, especially in open habitat, and were active by day
  while tigers were active at night. Tigers went where the prey was; leopards and dholes balanced
  food against safety [@Steinmetz2013].
- **By being displaced when tigers recover.** In Rajaji National Park, India, after herders and
  their livestock were moved out, tiger density rose from 3.31 to 5.81 per 100 km² in four years,
  while leopard density fell from 9.76 to 2.07. Leopards ate more livestock and small prey,
  meaning more conflict with farmers. The authors note the sample was small [@Harihar2011].
- **By timing.** GPS collars on six tigers and five leopards in Huai Kha Khaeng, Thailand, showed
  that leopards often used the same prey-rich places as tigers but at different times, rather
  than keeping to separate areas. Some pairs coexisted stably; in others the leopard shifted its
  range away [@Liu2026].
- **Diet overlap can be high.** In central Bhutan, tigers, leopards and dholes ate largely the
  same prey (sambar deer, muntjac, wild pig, and much livestock), with the two cats overlapping
  most [@Wang2009]. In Kalakkad-Mundanthurai, southern India, where prey is scarce, tigers mostly
  ate gaur while leopards and dholes took smaller prey [@Krishnakumar2022].
- In Nepal's Banke–Bardia parks, about 45% of the habitat suited to each cat was suited to both
  [@Kandel2025].

## Disagreements

- **Do tigers push leopards out?** In Nagarahole, with abundant prey, no spatial exclusion was
  found [@Karanth2000]; in Rajaji and in a prey-poor Thai forest, tigers did displace or deter
  them [@Harihar2011; @Steinmetz2013]. These are not in conflict if the answer depends on how much
  prey there is. The course teaches it that way: with plenty of prey and dense cover, the three
  share the forest; when prey is scarce, the tiger wins and the others move or change diet. This
  reading is the dossier's, approved by the owner on 2026-11-27.
